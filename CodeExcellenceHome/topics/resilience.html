Resilience Patterns

Timeouts, retries with backoff and jitter, circuit breakers, bulkheads, fallbacks and load shedding, simulated live (Polly, resilience4j, and friends).

Intermediate → Advanced~60 min studyBuilds on: Outbound Pipelines & InterceptorsUpdated 2026-11-03

The problem: one slow dependency takes everything down

Your checkout service has a pool of 40 workers (threads, connections, or async slots: it does not matter which). It handles 200 requests a second. One request in five also calls the recommendations service, which normally answers in 50 ms. At 10:00 recommendations does not fail: it hangs. No error, no 503, the socket simply stays open.

Each request that touches recommendations now holds a worker for as long as the call takes. With no timeout that is "forever", so the 40 workers fill up within a second or two. From then on, requests that have nothing to do with recommendations (login, cart, payment) queue behind them and time out too. The dependency was optional; the outage is total. This is a cascading failure, and it is the most common way that distributed systems go down.

Press play below and change the protection. The simulation runs 60 seconds of traffic with recommendations ("B") hanging from second 10 to second 40. Watch the pool, and watch what happens to the requests that never call B.

With the defaults, about half of the requests that never touch B fail during the outage when there is no timeout and one shared pool. A 1 s timeout limits the damage, because each stuck call now gives its worker back after a second, but B still occupies a share of the pool. A bulkhead (B may use at most 10 of the 40 workers) keeps every other request at 100%: B fails fast once its 10 slots are full, and everything else carries on. Timeout plus bulkhead also frees B's slots quickly, so B's own callers get faster errors.

Every pattern on this page exists to stop one of four things: a blip that turns into an error, a slow call that eats your capacity, a dead dependency that you keep calling, or too much load that you keep accepting.

Resilience is about time and load, not about errors. An error that comes back in 5 ms is cheap. A call that takes 30 s holds a thread, a socket, memory and a user's patience the whole time. Bound every wait (timeouts), bound every pool (bulkheads, rate limits, load shedding), retry only what is safe and only with jitter, and stop calling what is clearly down (circuit breakers). Then decide what the user sees instead (fallback).

This page is the sequel to Outbound HTTP Pipelines, where these strategies sit as handlers in the client's onion. Here we look inside each strategy: what it does to time and load, how to tune it, and where in the order it belongs.

The mental model: four kinds of trouble, one toolbox

Most production incidents involving a dependency fall into one of four shapes. Each strategy addresses a specific shape, and using the wrong one makes things worse: retrying an overloaded dependency, for example, adds more load to the thing that is already drowning.

Strategy Protects Costs Handles
Timeout your threads, sockets and the user's wait some calls that would have succeeded are cut off slow, hanging
Retry the success rate extra load on the dependency, extra latency blips
Circuit breaker the dependency (time to recover) and your latency some good calls are rejected while open down
Bulkhead the rest of your service from one dependency a hard cap on concurrency to that dependency slow, hanging
Rate limiter the dependency, or your quota with it requests rejected or delayed above the rate overload (caused by you)
Load shedding your own service some requests rejected early overload (caused by others)
Fallback the user experience a degraded or stale answer, plus code that is rarely exercised down, slow
Hedging tail latency extra load (a few percent if tuned) slow tail

Timeouts: the strategy everything else depends on

A timeout is the only strategy that turns "slow" into "failed", and every other strategy can act only on failures. A retry can't retry a call that never ends; a circuit breaker can't count a failure that never arrives. No timeout, no resilience.

Kinds of timeouts

There are several clocks in an outbound call. You need a bound on each of them, and they nest.

  • Connect timeout: how long to wait for TCP and TLS. It should be short (1–3 s inside a data centre): a host that does not accept a connection in a second is probably not there, and failing fast lets a retry go to a different instance.
  • Attempt timeout (also called a request or per-try timeout): one try, from sending to receiving the response headers (and usually the body). This is the one that makes retries possible.
  • Read / idle timeout: the maximum gap between bytes. It protects long streaming downloads, where a total timeout would be wrong, from a peer that stops sending halfway.
  • Total timeout (the deadline): the whole logical operation, including every attempt and every backoff. This is the number the caller cares about, and the one you should derive from your own SLO.

The defaults are not safe

Most HTTP clients ship with a timeout that is either missing or far too long for a service-to-service call. Know yours:

"None" means the call can wait until TCP gives up, which on Linux is about 15 minutes for an established connection whose peer vanished. Python requests without timeout= is the classic way a worker pool freezes silently. Linters can catch it: Ruff/Bandit rule S113 flags requests calls without a timeout.

Choosing the value

A timeout is a prediction: "if no answer has come by now, one is unlikely to come soon enough to be useful". Two inputs decide it:

  1. The dependency's latency distribution. Look at its p99 (or p99.9) under normal load. An attempt timeout slightly above it (say p99 × 1.5, or p99.9) cuts off only the calls that were genuinely stuck. Set it below the p99 and you time out 1% or more of healthy calls, and if you then retry them you add load exactly when the dependency is slowest.
  2. The caller's budget. If your own SLO is 2 s, a dependency call with a 5 s timeout is meaningless: by the time it fires, your caller has already given up. The total timeout of every downstream call must fit inside your remaining budget, which is the next idea.

Deadline propagation: stop working for someone who has left

Per-hop timeouts have a flaw: each hop chooses its own number, without knowing how much time its caller has left. The client gives up after 2 s, but the gateway allows 3 s, so does orders, and payments grinds on for another second, for a response that nobody will read. That wasted work is pure load, and it arrives exactly when the system is slow.

Deadline propagation fixes this by sending the absolute deadline (or the remaining budget) along with the request. Each hop subtracts what it has used and passes on the rest; when the deadline passes, every hop cancels together. gRPC does this automatically (the grpc-timeout header); Go's context.Context does it inside a process; over plain HTTP you do it yourself with a header.

With fixed per-hop timeouts, the default scenario wastes about 3 seconds of work after the client gave up (red segments). With a propagated deadline, every hop is cancelled at 2000 ms and nothing is wasted. Notice the minimum budget check: a hop that receives less than 100 ms of budget refuses immediately instead of starting work it cannot finish. That is cheap load shedding, and it is why you should also subtract a small margin for network time at each hop.

// Outbound: a DelegatingHandler forwards the remaining budget to the next hop.
public sealed class DeadlineHandler(TimeProvider clock) : DelegatingHandler
{
    protected override Task<HttpResponseMessage> SendAsync(HttpRequestMessage req, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        if (req.Options.TryGetValue(Deadline.Key, out var deadline))
        {
            var left = deadline - clock.GetUtcNow();
            if (left < TimeSpan.FromMilliseconds(100))
                throw new TimeoutRejectedException("Not enough budget left to start this call.");
            req.Headers.Add("x-request-timeout-ms", ((int)left.TotalMilliseconds).ToString());
        }
        return base.SendAsync(req, ct);
    }
}

async function getJson(url: string, deadline: number, signal: AbortSignal) {
  const left = deadline - Date.now();
  if (left < 100) throw new Error("not enough budget left to start this call");
  const res = await fetch(url, {
    signal,
    headers: { "x-request-timeout-ms": String(left) },
  });
  if (!res.ok) throw new HttpError(res.status);
  return res.json();
}

How the ecosystems differ, and why:

  • Go makes it the default: every blocking call takes a ctx, and WithTimeout can only shorten an inherited deadline. The discipline is to never use context.Background() inside a request.
  • .NET passes a CancellationToken instead of a deadline. Tokens compose (CreateLinkedTokenSource), but forgetting to pass one to a single await silently disables cancellation there; analyzer CA2016 warns about it.
  • Java has no ambient cancellation for blocking calls; gRPC's Context and reactive operators (Mono.timeout) carry it. With virtual threads and structured concurrency (StructuredTaskScope) this is getting closer to Go's model.
  • Python asyncio cancels by raising CancelledError into the task. asyncio.timeout turns that into TimeoutError at the boundary. Never swallow CancelledError.
  • TypeScript uses AbortSignal. AbortSignal.any and AbortSignal.timeout are available in all current runtimes; libraries must accept and forward a signal for cancellation to reach the socket.
  • gRPC propagates deadlines across processes for you: it sends the remaining time as grpc-timeout and the server's context is cancelled when it expires. Over HTTP there is no standard header; teams use something like x-request-timeout-ms (Envoy uses x-envoy-expected-rq-timeout-ms). Send a relative budget rather than an absolute timestamp, so clock skew between machines doesn't matter.

Retries: only the right failures, only with jitter

A retry is a bet that the next attempt will meet a different world: a different instance behind the load balancer, a lock that has been released, a leader that has been elected. For a blip that bet usually pays. For a dependency that is down or overloaded it doesn't: every retry is one more request against a service that can't serve the ones it already has.

Should I retry this?

A few details behind the boxes:

  • "It surely never left": if the connection was refused or DNS failed, the server never saw the request, so even a POST is safe to retry. Once any byte has been sent, you can't know. A timeout after sending is the dangerous case: the server may have done the work.
  • 500 is a judgement call. A 500 usually means a bug, and the same request will hit the same bug. Many teams retry 500 once at most, or not at all; the .NET standard handler retries all 5xx by default. 502, 503 and 504 are the classic transient responses from proxies and overloaded servers.
  • 429 means "you are sending too much": retry only after the time in Retry-After, never immediately, and never more aggressively than the server asked.
  • Never retry validation errors (400, 422), auth errors (401, 403: refresh the credential once, which is a different thing), 404, or 409 conflicts. The same request gives the same answer.

Backoff and jitter

When a dependency recovers from a one-second outage, every client that failed during that second wants to retry. If they all wait the same amount of time, they all come back at the same moment, and the recovering dependency sees a spike far above its capacity, fails again, and the cycle repeats. That is the thundering herd, and exponential backoff alone does not fix it: it spreads the retries out over time, but each wave is still synchronised.

The fix is jitter: randomise each delay so that the herd turns into a smooth trickle. Run the simulation below with 200 clients and a dependency that can serve 20 requests per 100 ms, then compare the strategies.

With the defaults (200 clients, a 1 s outage, 10 attempts each):

Strategy Delay before retry n Result in the simulation
Immediate 0 all 2,000 attempts are wasted: every client gives up
Fixed base most clients give up: the waves stay synchronised
Exponential, no jitter min(cap, base·2ⁿ) everyone succeeds, but only after about 33 s: the waves are synchronised and ever further apart
Full jitter random(0, min(cap, base·2ⁿ)) done in about 4 s
Equal jitter t/2 + random(0, t/2), with t = min(cap, base·2ⁿ) about 3 s; never retries "too early"
Decorrelated jitter min(cap, random(base, prev·3)) about 3 s; each delay depends on the previous one, not on n

The three jittered strategies are close to each other, and much better than any unjittered one. That matches the measurements in the AWS Architecture Blog post that popularised them. Which jitter you pick matters far less than having jitter. Use whatever your library offers: Polly's UseJitter (decorrelated for exponential backoff), cockatiel's default (decorrelated), tenacity's wait_random_exponential (full), Resilience4j's randomised exponential backoff (±50% around the exponential value).

Always cap the delay, and always cap the attempts. A cap of a few seconds (the delay) and 3–4 attempts (the count) is right for synchronous calls on behalf of a user. Background jobs and queue consumers can use longer caps and more attempts, because nobody is waiting, but they still need jitter: 10,000 messages that failed together will otherwise be retried together.

import random, time, email.utils

TRANSIENT_STATUS = {408, 429, 500, 502, 503, 504}

def full_jitter(attempt: int, base: float = 0.1, cap: float = 5.0) -> float:
    return random.uniform(0, min(cap, base * 2 ** attempt))

def retry_after_seconds(value: str | None) -> float | None:
    """Retry-After is either delay-seconds ("120") or an HTTP date."""
    if not value:
        return None
    if value.strip().isdigit():
        return float(value)
    try:
        when = email.utils.parsedate_to_datetime(value)
    except (TypeError, ValueError):
        return None
    return max(0.0, when.timestamp() - time.time())

def call_with_retry(send, *, max_attempts=4, deadline=None,
                    sleep=time.sleep, clock=time.monotonic):
    """send() makes ONE attempt. Wrap only idempotent operations."""
    for attempt in range(max_attempts):
        last = attempt == max_attempts - 1
        err = resp = None
        try:
            resp = send()
        except (ConnectionError, TimeoutError) as e:
            if last:
                raise
            err, delay = e, full_jitter(attempt)
        else:
            if resp.status_code not in TRANSIENT_STATUS or last:
                return resp
            hint = retry_after_seconds(resp.headers.get("Retry-After"))
            delay = hint if hint is not None else full_jitter(attempt)
        if deadline is not None and clock() + delay >= deadline:
            if err is not None:
                raise err                  # no time to retry: surface the real failure
            return resp
        sleep(delay)

var transient = map[int]bool{408: true, 429: true, 500: true, 502: true, 503: true, 504: true}

// Do retries an idempotent request. newReq must build a fresh request (and body) per attempt.
func Do(ctx context.Context, c *http.Client, newReq func(context.Context) (*http.Request, error), maxAttempts int) (*http.Response, error) {
    for attempt := 0; ; attempt++ {
        req, err := newReq(ctx)
        if err != nil {
            return nil, err
        }
        res, err := c.Do(req)
        last := attempt == maxAttempts-1
        var delay time.Duration
        switch {
        case err != nil:
            if last || ctx.Err() != nil { // the caller has given up: don't retry for nobody
                return nil, err
            }
            delay = fullJitter(attempt)
        case !transient[res.StatusCode] || last:
            return res, nil
        default:
            delay = retryAfter(res.Header.Get("Retry-After"))
            if delay <= 0 {
                delay = fullJitter(attempt)
            }
            if !fits(ctx, delay) {
                return res, nil // no time left: hand back the real 503
            }
            io.Copy(io.Discard, io.LimitReader(res.Body, 64<<10))
            res.Body.Close() // otherwise every retry leaks a connection
        }
        if !fits(ctx, delay) {
            return nil, err
        }
        t := time.NewTimer(delay)
        select {
        case <-ctx.Done():
            t.Stop()
            return nil, ctx.Err()
        case <-t.C:
        }
    }
}

func fullJitter(attempt int) time.Duration {
    const base, ceiling = 100 * time.Millisecond, 5 * time.Second
    d := ceiling
    if attempt < 6 {
        d = min(ceiling, base<<attempt)
    }
    return rand.N(d + 1) // math/rand/v2
}

func retryAfter(v string) time.Duration {
    if secs, err := strconv.Atoi(v); err == nil {
        return time.Duration(secs) * time.Second
    }
    if t, err := http.ParseTime(v); err == nil {
        return time.Until(t)
    }
    return 0
}

func fits(ctx context.Context, d time.Duration) bool {
    dl, ok := ctx.Deadline()
    return !ok || time.Until(dl) > d
}

const TRANSIENT = new Set([408, 429, 500, 502, 503, 504]);

const fullJitter = (attempt: number, base = 100, cap = 5_000) =>
  Math.random() * Math.min(cap, base * 2 ** attempt);

function retryAfterMs(v: string | null): number | undefined {
  if (!v) return undefined;
  if (/^\d+$/.test(v.trim())) return Number(v) * 1000;
  const at = Date.parse(v);                       // HTTP date form
  return Number.isNaN(at) ? undefined : Math.max(0, at - Date.now());
}

const sleep = (ms: number, signal?: AbortSignal) =>
  new Promise<void>((resolve, reject) => {
    const t = setTimeout(resolve, ms);
    signal?.addEventListener("abort", () => { clearTimeout(t); reject(signal.reason); }, { once: true });
  });

/** Retries an idempotent request. Pass the caller's deadline and signal. */
export async function fetchWithRetry(url: string, init: RequestInit & { deadline: number },
                                     maxAttempts = 4): Promise<Response> {
  for (let attempt = 0; ; attempt++) {
    const last = attempt === maxAttempts - 1;
    let delay: number;
    try {
      const res = await fetch(url, init);
      if (!TRANSIENT.has(res.status) || last) return res;
      delay = retryAfterMs(res.headers.get("retry-after")) ?? fullJitter(attempt);
      if (Date.now() + delay >= init.deadline) return res;    // no time left: return the real 503
      await res.body?.cancel();                               // release the connection
    } catch (err) {
      if (last || init.signal?.aborted) throw err;            // the caller gave up
      delay = fullJitter(attempt);
      if (Date.now() + delay >= init.deadline) throw err;
    }
    await sleep(delay, init.signal ?? undefined);
  }
}

Things worth noticing in all five:

  • The attempt is a function (send, newReq, fetch(url, init)) that can be called again. A request body that is a stream can't be replayed; buffer it, or don't retry.
  • The loop checks the deadline before sleeping. A retry that can't finish in time is pure waste, and returning the real failure (503, the original exception) is more useful to the caller than a generic timeout.
  • Go and TypeScript drain or cancel the body of a discarded response. Otherwise each retry leaks a connection from the pool, which is exactly the resource you are short of during an incident.

Retry amplification: why only one layer may retry

Retries compose by multiplication. If the edge retries 3 times, and each attempt calls service A which retries 3 times, and each of those calls B which retries 3 times, then one user click becomes up to 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 calls on the database at the bottom. The database is usually the thing that is struggling in the first place.

With the leaf failing 100% of the time, "every layer retries" produces exactly 27 database calls per click, and the simulation's average matches the exact formula. Switch to "only the edge retries" or "only the last hop retries" and it drops to 3. Lower the failure rate to 10% and the difference nearly disappears, because most calls succeed first time. Amplification only bites during an incident, which is why it hides so well in normal testing.

The fixes, from simplest to most robust:

  1. Retry at one layer per hop, and fail fast everywhere else. Usually that is the layer closest to the failing dependency (it knows the most about which errors are transient), or the edge (it knows the user's deadline). Pick one and write it down. SDKs count as a layer: the Azure SDK, AWS SDK and gRPC client all retry by default.
  2. Tell the caller not to retry. If you already retried and failed, return an error that says so. Google's SRE guidance is to return an "overloaded; don't retry" status so that upstream layers fail fast. In HTTP, a 503 without Retry-After from a server that has already retried is a common convention, but it is a convention: document it.
  3. Retry budgets. Limit retries to a fraction of normal traffic, per client. When most calls succeed, the budget is full and an occasional retry is free. When most calls fail, the budget empties and retries stop, so a hard outage costs about 1× load instead of 27×. Switch the simulation to "every layer + retry budget" to see it.

class RetryBudget:
    """gRPC-style retry throttling: failures cost 1 token, successes earn
    `ratio`; retries are allowed only while the bucket is more than half full."""
    def __init__(self, max_tokens: float = 10, ratio: float = 0.1):
        self.max, self.ratio, self.tokens = max_tokens, ratio, float(max_tokens)
        self._lock = threading.Lock()

    def record(self, ok: bool) -> None:
        with self._lock:
            self.tokens = (min(self.max, self.tokens + self.ratio) if ok
                           else max(0.0, self.tokens - 1))

    def can_retry(self) -> bool:
        with self._lock:
            return self.tokens > self.max / 2

# In the retry loop: record() after every attempt; before sleeping, check
# can_retry() and give up early if it says no. One budget per downstream.

Honour Retry-After

Retry-After is the server telling you exactly when to come back. It appears on 429 Too Many Requests and 503 Service Unavailable (and on 3xx redirects, which is a different use), as either a number of seconds or an HTTP date. Honouring it is not optional politeness: a server that sends it is usually shedding load, and a client that ignores it is part of the reason.

  • If Retry-After is longer than your remaining budget, don't wait: return the 429/503 to your caller (or fall back) now.
  • Add a little jitter on top of it. Every client that got Retry-After: 2 at the same moment will otherwise come back at the same moment.
  • Treat it as a minimum, not an invitation: you may wait longer, never shorter.
  • Rate-limit headers (RateLimit-Policy and RateLimit from the IETF draft, or the older X-RateLimit-Remaining) let a client slow down before it is rejected. If your dependency sends them, a client-side rate limiter (see Rate limiting) can use them.

Idempotency: make the retried request safe

A retry is only safe if doing the operation twice has the same effect as doing it once. GET, PUT and DELETE are idempotent by definition in HTTP (if the server implements them correctly). POST and PATCH are not: "charge this card €50" twice is two charges.

The dangerous moment is a timeout after the request was sent. The client can't tell "the request never arrived" from "it was processed and the response was lost" from "it is still being processed". An idempotency key removes the ambiguity: the client generates a unique key per logical operation (not per attempt), sends it on every attempt, and the server stores the result under that key. A retry with the same key gets the stored result instead of a second charge.

Walk through each scenario with and without the key. With the key, every scenario ends with exactly one charge. Without it, "response lost" and "client times out while the server still works" both charge twice, and nothing in the client's logs reveals it. The fourth scenario shows why the server must also store a fingerprint of the request body: a retry that reuses a key with a different body is a client bug, and 422 is safer than silently returning the first result.

The server's rules, which the visualizer follows:

  1. Insert the key first, atomically, as "in progress" (a unique constraint or INSERT ... ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING). Two concurrent attempts can't both win.
  2. Key exists and is completed with the same body hash: return the stored response (status and body), without doing the work.
  3. Key exists and is in progress: return 409 Conflict (with Retry-After), so the client retries later instead of starting a second charge.
  4. Key exists with a different body hash: return 422.
  5. Do the work and store the response in the same transaction as the side effect, where you can. If the side effect is in another system (a payment provider), pass the key on to it: Stripe, Adyen and most payment APIs accept their own idempotency key for exactly this reason.
  6. Expire keys after a window that is longer than any client will keep retrying (Stripe uses 24 hours).

Circuit breakers: stop calling what is clearly down

Retries handle blips. When a dependency is down, every call to it still costs a timeout's worth of waiting, and every one of those calls is load on something trying to restart. A circuit breaker watches the outcomes of recent calls and, when too many fail, opens: for a while, calls are rejected instantly without touching the network. Then it lets a few probe calls through to see whether the dependency has recovered.

Pick a dependency behaviour and run it with and without the breaker. The chart shows the failure rate in the sliding window against the threshold; the strip underneath shows each call: succeeded, failed (after waiting), or short-circuited: rejected instantly by the breaker (see the legend).

What to look for:

  • Hard outage: without a breaker, every call during the outage waits and fails. With one, after the first handful of failures the calls fail in microseconds, and a probe every few seconds discovers the recovery.
  • Overloaded, heals after 1.5 s of quiet: this is the case breakers exist for. Without a breaker the dependency never gets 1.5 s of quiet, so it never recovers. With one, the open period gives it room. Set "open for (s)" to 1 and watch it fail to recover even with the breaker: the break duration must be longer than the dependency needs to recover.
  • Flaky, 30% failures: a breaker is not a tool for partial failure, but random clusters can still trip it. With a 20-call window, some seeds push the window over 50% and the breaker opens for no good reason; with a 50-call window it stays closed. Lower the threshold to 20% and it opens repeatedly, rejecting calls of which 70% would have succeeded.
  • Short blip: with a 20-call window, the 0.6 s blip is only a few failures out of 20, and nothing trips. Shrink the window to 6 calls (min calls 3) and the same blip opens the breaker: 3 real failures become about 30 rejected calls. Small windows and low minimum throughput make breakers jumpy.

Consecutive-failure breakers (open after N failures in a row: gobreaker's default is more than 5, cockatiel's ConsecutiveBreaker) are simple and fine at low traffic. Ratio breakers over a window (Polly, Resilience4j, cockatiel's SamplingBreaker) are better at high traffic, where a few failures in a row are normal noise.

Key breakers by the thing that fails together, usually host (or host + route class). In .NET, AddStandardResilienceHandler gives each named client its own pipeline; SelectPipelineByAuthority() gives one per host. Breakers are also per process: 50 pods each learn about an outage separately, which is normally fine (it costs each pod a handful of calls). Gobreaker's DistributedCircuitBreaker shares state through a store, if you really need that.

Bulkheads: limit what one dependency can take from you

The cascade at the top of this page is the problem bulkheads solve. A ship's bulkheads stop one flooded compartment from sinking the ship; a software bulkhead stops one slow dependency from occupying every worker. It is a concurrency limit per dependency: at most N calls in flight, optionally a small queue, and everything beyond is rejected immediately.

  • Size it from Little's law: concurrent calls ≈ request rate × latency. 50 calls/s at 100 ms is 5 in flight on average; a limit of 10–20 leaves room for spikes. When latency rises tenfold, the limit is what stops 50 in flight turning into 500.
  • Queue little or not at all. A queue in front of a bulkhead is waiting time, and waiting time is what you are trying to bound. Polly's concurrency limiter and the .NET standard handler use a queue of 0 by default.
  • Choose between rejecting and degrading. A rejected call is a fast failure, so it can go straight to a fallback.
  • Thread-pool bulkheads (a separate pool per dependency, as in Hystrix and Resilience4j's ThreadPoolBulkhead) also isolate CPU and blocking I/O, at the cost of context switches. Semaphore bulkheads are cheaper and are the default almost everywhere now, especially with async I/O, virtual threads and goroutines, where threads are not the scarce resource.

Rate limiting: protect a dependency, or a quota

Rate limiting answers "how much traffic is allowed per unit of time?". There are two very different users of it:

  • Server side, to protect yourself from clients. That is middleware, covered in Middleware Patterns (ASP.NET Core's AddRateLimiter, Spring with Bucket4j, slowapi, x/time/rate in a handler, express-rate-limit).
  • Client side, to stay under someone else's limit: a payment provider's 100 requests/s, a partner API's daily quota, a database you promised not to hammer. This belongs in the outbound pipeline, outside the retry, so that retries count towards the limit too.

Both use the same small set of algorithms, and they differ most in how they treat bursts. Compare them on the same traffic:

Try "burst at the window boundary": the fixed window admits 20 requests in two ticks against a limit of 10 per window, because the counter resets in between. The sliding log and the sliding window counter both hold the line at 10. Try "bursty": the token bucket admits a burst up to B and then settles at the refill rate, while the leaky bucket queues the burst and drains it at a constant rate, so nothing is rejected until the queue is full, but everything waits.

Load shedding: say no early, to the right requests

Rate limiting caps traffic at a number you chose in advance. Load shedding responds to your actual capacity right now: when you are overloaded, reject some work immediately so that the rest completes in time. The insight is that during overload, a request that is served too late is worse than one rejected at once: it used capacity and failed.

Run the spike below with each policy. Capacity is 10 requests per tick, normal traffic is 8, the spike brings 18 for 20 ticks, and clients give up after 10 ticks.

With an unbounded FIFO queue, the queue grows during the spike, every request waits longer than the client's timeout, and about half of all the work done is wasted on requests nobody is waiting for anymore (374 useful against 376 wasted with the defaults). The service is 100% busy and nearly useless. A bounded queue rejects the excess at the door and almost doubles the useful work. LIFO (serve the newest first) keeps recent requests fresh while old ones expire. Dropping expired requests before working on them does best of all here: it never spends capacity on a request whose client has left.

Techniques that real systems use:

  • Bound every queue, including the ones you don't think of as queues: the server's accept backlog, thread-pool queues, channel buffers, Kafka consumer lag.
  • Carry the deadline (see deadline propagation) and check it when the work is dequeued, not only when it arrives.
  • Shed by priority: health checks and paying users' checkout before analytics and prefetches. Mark low-priority traffic with a header so that every layer can shed it first.
  • Adaptive LIFO and CoDel: Facebook's servers switch the queue to LIFO under load, and CoDel-style queues reject when the minimum queueing delay over an interval stays above a target (for example 5 ms over 100 ms), which separates a standing queue from a short burst.
  • Client-side adaptive throttling (Google SRE): each client tracks requests and accepts over two minutes and rejects locally with probability max(0, (requests − K·accepts) / (requests + 1)), with K = 2. When the backend rejects a lot, the clients stop sending most of the traffic before it leaves their process.
  • In .NET, System.Threading.RateLimiting.ConcurrencyLimiter with a small queue in ASP.NET Core's rate-limiting middleware is a simple concurrency-based shedder; Kestrel's MaxConcurrentConnections is a blunt one.

Fallbacks: decide what the user sees instead

When the call fails for good (retries exhausted, breaker open, bulkhead full, deadline gone), something still has to be returned. A fallback is that decision, made in advance rather than as an unhandled exception.

Fallback Example Watch out for
Stale data last known prices from a cache, flagged as stale how stale is acceptable? See stale-while-revalidate in Caching
A static default empty recommendations; a generic banner hiding a real outage from your dashboards: count fallbacks

Two rules. A fallback must be cheaper and more reliable than the thing it replaces: a fallback that calls another remote service under load is how fires spread. And it must be exercised: fallback code that runs only during incidents is the least-tested code you own, so test it (see Testing resilience) and alert on how often it fires.

Never fall back silently on writes or money. "Payment service down, so mark the order as paid and sort it out later" is a business decision with fraud and accounting consequences, not a resilience setting. Fallbacks for reads are usually safe; fallbacks for writes need a product owner's sign-off.

Hedging: buy a shorter tail with a little extra load

Some slow calls aren't failures. They are the tail: a GC pause, a cold cache, a noisy neighbour on one replica. Retrying after a timeout fixes them too late, because you have already waited for the timeout. Hedging sends a second copy of the request to another replica if the first hasn't answered within a short delay d (typically around the p95), uses whichever answers first, and cancels the other.

With 3% slow responses, the defaults give a p99 of about 750 ms without hedging. Hedging after 50 ms brings the p99 down to about 75 ms for only about 4% extra requests, because 96% of calls finish before the hedge would be sent. "Always send 2" cuts the tail a little further, but doubles the load, and with load sensitivity switched on the extra load makes the median worse. Google's "The Tail at Scale" reports the same shape on a real system: hedging after the p95 cut the 99.9th percentile from 1,800 ms to 74 ms for 2% more requests.

Hedging is only safe when:

  • the operation is idempotent (two copies may both run to completion),
  • the copies go to different replicas (two requests to the same overloaded instance help nobody),
  • the hedge delay is adaptive or at least set from measured latency (around p95), and hedges are capped (a budget, as for retries),
  • you cancel the loser, so the extra load lasts only as long as needed.

The .NET standard hedging handler (AddStandardHedgingHandler) is built for this; gRPC has a hedgingPolicy in the service config (a call can have a retry policy or a hedging policy, not both). Don't combine hedging with an aggressive retry on the same call: you get both multipliers.

Putting it together: the order of strategies

Each strategy is a layer of middleware around the call, exactly like the outbound handler chain: the outer layer sees one logical call, the inner layers see every attempt. So the order changes the behaviour, not just the style. Here is the order nearly every mature library converges on, outermost first:

# Layer Why it sits here
1 Fallback Outermost, so it catches everything that escapes: the final error after retries, an open breaker, the total timeout, a full bulkhead. Put it inside the retry and the first error becomes a "success" that nothing retries.
2 Total timeout The caller's budget for the whole operation, including every retry and every backoff. Without it, 4 attempts × 1 s + backoff = 4.4 s, not the 3 s you promised.
3 Bulkhead / concurrency limit One permit per logical call, so a retry sequence doesn't need a new slot (and can't deadlock waiting for one). Rejected calls fail in microseconds. The .NET standard handler puts its rate limiter here, outermost of all.
4 Retry Outside the breaker, so each attempt goes through the breaker: the breaker counts every failure, and once it opens, the next attempt short-circuits instead of hitting the dependency.
5 Circuit breaker Sees each attempt. Inside the retry, an open breaker turns "4 slow attempts" into "1 instant rejection".
6 Attempt timeout Innermost: it bounds one try, so a hung attempt is abandoned in time for a retry to run within the total budget.

import asyncio, time, httpx
from tenacity import AsyncRetrying, stop_after_attempt, wait_random_exponential, retry_if_exception

class Rejected(Exception): ...

class Breaker:
    """Opens after n consecutive failures; lets a probe through after cool_down."""
    def __init__(self, n=5, cool_down=5.0):
        self.n, self.cool_down, self.fails, self.opened_at = n, cool_down, 0, None
    def check(self):
        if self.opened_at and time.monotonic() - self.opened_at < self.cool_down:
            raise Rejected("breaker open")
    def record(self, ok):
        self.fails = 0 if ok else self.fails + 1
        self.opened_at = time.monotonic() if self.fails >= self.n else None

def transient(e):
    if isinstance(e, (httpx.TransportError, TimeoutError)):
        return True
    return isinstance(e, httpx.HTTPStatusError) and e.response.status_code in (408, 429, 500, 502, 503, 504)

breaker, bulkhead = Breaker(), asyncio.Semaphore(20)

async def one_attempt(client, url):
    breaker.check()                                   # open: fail fast, not retried
    try:
        async with asyncio.timeout(1.0):              # attempt timeout (innermost)
            r = await client.get(url)
            r.raise_for_status()
    except Exception as e:
        breaker.record(ok=not transient(e))           # a 404 is not the dependency's fault
        raise
    breaker.record(ok=True)
    return r.json()

async def get_price(client, url, cached):
    try:
        if bulkhead.locked():
            raise Rejected("bulkhead full")           # reject, don't queue
        async with bulkhead, asyncio.timeout(3.0):    # total timeout covers every retry
            async for attempt in AsyncRetrying(
                    stop=stop_after_attempt(4),
                    wait=wait_random_exponential(multiplier=0.2, max=2),
                    retry=retry_if_exception(transient), reraise=True):
                with attempt:
                    return await one_attempt(client, url)
    except Exception as e:                            # fallback (outermost)
        if isinstance(e, Rejected) or transient(e):
            return cached
        raise                                         # a 404 or 400 is a real answer

type StatusError struct{ Code int } // what fetch returns for a non-2xx response (Error() omitted)

func isTransient(err error) bool {
    var se *StatusError
    if errors.As(err, &se) {
        return transient[se.Code]
    }
    var ne net.Error
    return errors.As(err, &ne) || errors.Is(err, context.DeadlineExceeded)
}

type PriceClient struct {
    fetch   func(ctx context.Context, id string) (*Price, error)
    cache   *PriceCache
    slots   chan struct{} // bulkhead: make(chan struct{}, 20)
    breaker *gobreaker.CircuitBreaker[*Price]
}

func newBreaker() *gobreaker.CircuitBreaker[*Price] {
    return gobreaker.NewCircuitBreaker[*Price](gobreaker.Settings{
        Name:        "prices",
        MaxRequests: 3,                // probes allowed while half-open
        Interval:    10 * time.Second, // closed state: reset the counts every 10 s
        Timeout:     5 * time.Second,  // open -> half-open
        ReadyToTrip: func(c gobreaker.Counts) bool {
            return c.Requests >= 20 && float64(c.TotalFailures)/float64(c.Requests) >= 0.5
        },
        IsSuccessful: func(err error) bool { return err == nil || !isTransient(err) }, // 404 != failure
    })
}

func (c *PriceClient) Get(ctx context.Context, id string) (*Price, error) {
    select { // bulkhead: reject at once when full
    case c.slots <- struct{}{}:
        defer func() { <-c.slots }()
    default:
        return c.cache.Last(id), nil
    }
    ctx, cancel := context.WithTimeout(ctx, 3*time.Second) // total timeout
    defer cancel()

    var err error
    for attempt := 0; attempt < 4; attempt++ {
        if attempt > 0 && !sleep(ctx, fullJitter(attempt)) {
            break
        }
        var p *Price
        p, err = c.breaker.Execute(func() (*Price, error) {
            actx, cancel := context.WithTimeout(ctx, time.Second) // attempt timeout
            defer cancel()
            return c.fetch(actx, id)
        })
        if err == nil {
            return p, nil
        }
        if !isTransient(err) || ctx.Err() != nil {
            break // open breaker (ErrOpenState), a 4xx, or out of total time
        }
    }
    if isTransient(err) || errors.Is(err, gobreaker.ErrOpenState) || errors.Is(err, gobreaker.ErrTooManyRequests) {
        return c.cache.Last(id), nil // fallback
    }
    return nil, err
}

func sleep(ctx context.Context, d time.Duration) bool {
    t := time.NewTimer(d)
    defer t.Stop()
    select {
    case <-ctx.Done():
        return false
    case <-t.C:
        return true
    }
}

class HttpStatusError extends Error {
  constructor(public status: number) { super(`HTTP ${status}`); }
}

const isTransient = (e: Error) =>
  e instanceof HttpStatusError ? [408, 429, 500, 502, 503, 504].includes(e.status)
  : !isBrokenCircuitError(e) && !isBulkheadRejectedError(e);

// wrap(a, b, c): a is the OUTERMOST.
const pipeline = wrap(
  bulkhead(20, 0),                                    // 20 in flight, no queue
  timeout(3_000, TimeoutStrategy.Aggressive),         // total timeout
  retry(handleWhen(isTransient), {
    maxAttempts: 3,                                   // 3 retries = 4 calls
    backoff: new ExponentialBackoff({ initialDelay: 200, maxDelay: 2_000 }), // decorrelated jitter
  }),
  circuitBreaker(handleWhen(isTransient), {
    halfOpenAfter: 5_000,
    breaker: new SamplingBreaker({ threshold: 0.5, duration: 10_000, minimumRps: 2 }),
  }),
  timeout(1_000, TimeoutStrategy.Aggressive),         // attempt timeout
);

export async function getPrice(id: string, cached: Price): Promise<Price> {
  try {
    return await pipeline.execute(async ({ signal }) => {
      const res = await fetch(`https://prices.internal/api/${id}`, { signal });
      if (!res.ok) throw new HttpStatusError(res.status);
      return (await res.json()) as Price;
    });
  } catch (e) {                                       // fallback (outermost)
    const err = e as Error;
    if (isTransient(err) || isBrokenCircuitError(err) || isBulkheadRejectedError(err) || isTaskCancelledError(err)) return cached;
    throw err;
  }
}

The standard pipelines in .NET

For HttpClient you rarely build the pipeline yourself. AddStandardResilienceHandler() from Microsoft.Extensions.Http.Resilience adds five strategies, outermost first:

1 Rate limiter Concurrency limiter: 1,000 permits, queue 0
2 Total request timeout 30 s
3 Retry 3 retries, exponential with jitter, 2 s base delay; honours Retry-After; handles 408, 429, 5xx, HttpRequestException and attempt timeouts

        o.AttemptTimeout.Timeout = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(1);
        o.TotalRequestTimeout.Timeout = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(3);
        o.Retry.MaxRetryAttempts = 3;
        o.Retry.Delay = TimeSpan.FromMilliseconds(200);
        o.Retry.DisableForUnsafeHttpMethods();             // no retries for POST, PATCH, PUT, DELETE, CONNECT
        o.CircuitBreaker.SamplingDuration = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(10); // must be >= 2 x attempt timeout

// Hedging instead of retries, for latency-sensitive idempotent reads:
builder.Services.AddHttpClient<SearchClient>().AddStandardHedgingHandler();

The options are validated at startup: the total timeout must exceed the attempt timeout, and the breaker's sampling duration must be at least twice the attempt timeout. The hedging handler has a different shape: total timeout (30 s) › hedging (up to 10 hedges, 2 s delay) › then, per endpoint, rate limiter › breaker › attempt timeout (10 s), so one bad replica's breaker opens without blocking hedges to the others. Two traps: don't stack your own AddResilienceHandler on top of the standard one (two retry layers), and if you set defaults for every client with ConfigureHttpClientDefaults, use RemoveAllResilienceHandlers() on the client that needs something different. SelectPipelineByAuthority() gives each host its own breaker, so a.example.com failing doesn't open the breaker for b.example.com.

Timeout Always, on every remote call: connect, attempt and total Never skip it. Be careful setting it below the p99.9, or long-running work will fail every time
Retry Transient errors (connect failures, 503, 429 with Retry-After) on idempotent operations Writes without idempotency keys; 4xx; when an inner layer already retries; when you're out of budget or deadline
Circuit breaker A dependency that fails hard and in bulk (down, overloaded), with enough traffic to measure Low-traffic calls (the window never fills); random partial failure; one breaker shared across hosts that fail independently
Bulkhead Several dependencies share one pool of threads, connections or memory A single-dependency service where the limit just adds a second queue
Rate limit (client) Partner quotas and APIs that charge per call As a substitute for the server protecting itself
Load shedding Servers that can be overloaded; queues with a timeout upstream Batch work, where queuing is the point
Fallback Reads with a reasonable degraded answer (cache, default, a smaller feature) Writes, money, security decisions; fallbacks that call another fragile service
Hedging Idempotent, latency-critical reads with several replicas Writes; a single replica; an overloaded backend (it adds load)

When NOT to retry

  • The error is not transient: 400, 401, 403, 404, 409, 422. The next attempt gets the same answer.
  • The operation is not idempotent and has no idempotency key: POST /payments, "send email", "increment counter".
  • Someone else already retries: an SDK, a service mesh, a gRPC service config, a queue's redelivery. One owner per hop.
  • The caller has gone: the deadline is spent or the request was cancelled. Retrying works for no one.
  • The dependency told you to stop: an open breaker, a 429 with a Retry-After beyond your deadline, or a 503 during a declared overload.
  • Inside a loop that already runs again: a consumer that will redeliver the message, or a scheduled job that runs every minute anyway.
  • Under a database transaction or a lock: holding them for 2 s of backoff turns one slow call into a blocked table. Retry the whole unit of work outside the transaction instead.

Testing resilience

Resilience code runs rarely and only when things are already going wrong, so it rots unless tests run it. Four levels, cheapest first:

  1. Unit-test the policy with a fake clock. Polly v8 strategies read time from TimeProvider; inject FakeTimeProvider (from Microsoft.Extensions.TimeProvider.Testing) and advance it, so a test of "breaker opens, then half-opens after 5 s" runs in milliseconds. In Go, inject the clock or keep delays as fields; in Python, patch tenacity's sleep; in TypeScript, use fake timers.
  2. Unit-test the order with a scripted fake dependency. A stub that answers "503, 503, 200" or "hang" and records each attempt (exactly what the pipeline explorer above does). Assert the number of attempts, the elapsed time against the total budget, and that a 404 is not retried. This one test catches every wrong order in the table.
  3. Inject faults in a running system. Polly's chaos strategies (AddChaosLatency, AddChaosFault, AddChaosOutcome, AddChaosBehavior), enabled by configuration in a test environment; or a proxy such as Toxiproxy between the service and a real dependency; or service-mesh fault injection. Watch the timeouts fire and the breaker open for real.
  4. Game days. Turn a dependency off in staging (or, carefully, in production) and check that the user-visible behaviour is the one you designed: the fallback shows, alerts fire, and recovery happens without a deploy.

Observe it in production. Every strategy should emit telemetry: retries per call, breaker state changes, rejections by the bulkhead and rate limiter, timeouts, and how often the fallback fires. Polly and Microsoft.Extensions.Resilience emit these as metrics and logs out of the box (resilience.polly.strategy.events and friends); Resilience4j has Micrometer modules. Alert on "breaker open" and on fallback rate, and put retry counts next to error rates on the dashboard: a rising retry rate is the earliest sign of a dependency in trouble. Pitfalls and war stories

Pitfall: retrying a POST A checkout service retried every failed POST /charges on timeout. The payment provider had processed the first request; only the response was lost. Customers were charged twice, about 40 times a day, all during a network blip. Fix: an Idempotency-Key on every write, a server that stores and replays results, and DisableForUnsafeHttpMethods() until both are in place.

Pitfall: a breaker that counts 4xx A new mobile release sent a malformed field, and its 400 responses were counted as failures. The breaker opened and rejected every call to the profile service, including the valid ones from older app versions and the web site. One client's bug became everyone's outage. Fix: count only transient failures (5xx, 408, 429, timeouts, connection errors) as breaker failures.

Pitfall: a timeout just below the p99 The team set a 200 ms timeout because "the p95 is 150 ms" and added 3 retries. Every slow-but-fine request now made 4 calls; the extra load pushed the p99 higher, which caused more timeouts. Fix: set the attempt timeout from the p99.9 plus headroom, cap retries with a budget, and consider hedging for the tail instead.

Pitfall: the fallback that had never run The recommendations fallback read from a Redis cache, whose key format had changed six months earlier. The first time recommendations went down, the fallback threw a deserialization exception and took the home page with it. Fix: test fallbacks (chaos strategies in staging), and emit a metric each time they run so you know they work.

Pitfall: one breaker for many hosts A client called 12 shards through one named HttpClient with one circuit breaker. One shard failed, its failures crossed the 10% ratio, and the breaker cut off all 12. Fix: one breaker per independent failure domain: SelectPipelineByAuthority(), a breaker per host in Go and Java, or the hedging handler's per-endpoint breakers.

Best-practice checklist

Timeouts everywhere

Attempt and total, on every call. Derive them from measured latency and the caller's budget, never the library default.

Propagate deadlines

Pass the remaining budget downstream (ctx, CancellationToken, grpc-timeout); stop when it is spent.

Retry rarely, with jitter

Transient errors, idempotent operations, one layer per hop, exponential backoff with jitter, a retry budget.

Make writes safe

Idempotency keys stored atomically with the result; replay, don't redo.

Break per failure domain

Breakers on failure ratio with a minimum throughput, counting only transient failures, one per host.

Bound concurrency

Bulkheads per dependency; shed load early on the server, using deadlines and priorities.

Decide the fallback

A product decision, cheap and local, tested, and visible on a dashboard.

Get the order right

Fallback › total timeout › bulkhead › retry › breaker › attempt timeout, pinned by a test.

  • [ ] Every outbound call has an attempt timeout and a total timeout, and passes the incoming deadline down.
  • [ ] Exactly one layer retries each hop; SDK, mesh and gRPC retries are accounted for.
  • [ ] Non-idempotent operations are not retried, or carry an idempotency key the server enforces.
  • [ ] Breakers ignore 4xx and are scoped per host; each dependency has a concurrency limit.
  • [ ] Dashboards show retries, breaker state, rejections and fallback rate; "breaker open" alerts someone.
  • [ ] A test pins the pipeline order and the behaviour for "503, 503, 200", "hang" and "404".

Self-check

Your attempt timeout is 1 s, you allow 3 retries with backoff 200, 400, 800 ms, and the caller gives you 3 s. The dependency hangs. When does the caller get an answer, and how many attempts were made?

At 3 s, from the total timeout, after 3 attempts (1 s + 0.2 s + 1 s + 0.4 s = 2.6 s, and the third attempt is cut off at 3 s). The fourth attempt never starts. Without a total timeout, it would be 4 × 1 s + 1.4 s of backoff = 4.4 s, and the caller would already have given up.

Three layers (gateway, service, data client) each retry 3 times (4 attempts). The database is down. How many calls does it receive per user request?

4 × 4 × 4 = 64. Retries multiply across layers. Retry at one layer only (usually the one closest to the failing dependency), and use retry budgets so that, under a broad outage, retries add at most about 10% to the load.

A circuit breaker sits outside the retry. The dependency is down. What changes compared with the recommended order?

The breaker sees only the result of the whole retry loop, so 4 failed attempts count as 1 failure. It takes about 4 times as many failed user calls before it opens, and each of them makes 4 attempts against a dependency that is down. Inside the retry, the breaker counts each attempt and, once open, short-circuits the remaining retries.

The client times out on POST /orders. Is it safe to retry? What has to be true?

Only if the request carries an idempotency key and the server enforces it: it stores the key with the result in the same transaction, replays the stored result for a repeated key, returns 409 while the first request is still in progress, and rejects a reused key with a different body. A timeout means "unknown", not "failed": the first request may have succeeded.

The service is overloaded and requests wait 4 s in a queue, but every caller times out after 2 s. Why is throughput of useful work falling, and what are two fixes?

The server is spending most of its capacity on requests whose callers have already left: it does the work, and nobody receives the answer. Fixes: bound the queue (reject early with 503 and Retry-After), and drop requests whose deadline has passed before doing the work (or serve LIFO under overload). In the shedding simulator, dropping expired work doubles the useful throughput.

When does hedging make latency worse instead of better?

When the backend is slow because it's overloaded: hedges add load, which makes everything slower, the median included. Also when both copies go to the same replica, when the hedge delay is lower than typical latency (every call is doubled), or when the losers aren't cancelled. Hedge at about the p95, cap the hedge rate, and only for idempotent reads.
